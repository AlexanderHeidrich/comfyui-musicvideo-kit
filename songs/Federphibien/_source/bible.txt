REFERENCE - read this first
  Seven images are supplied. Four are subjects, one is the look, two are places.

  <Picture 1> is a DESIGN SHEET, not a character standing by the pond. The creature
  on it does not exist in the world of the film. It appears only inside the chorus
  daydreams and on the researcher's page, in the shots that say so in their own
  words. If this shot does not name it, it is not in this shot - do not put it in
  the water, in the grass, in the hen house or anywhere in frame. What the film is
  about until the chorus is one ordinary frog.

  THE FEDERPHIBIUM, the hybrid child: a fat, potbellied sage-green frog body
  sitting SQUAT on the ground the way a toad sits, with a large pale cream belly and
  soft mottled olive patches over the green. An upright red chicken comb on its
  head, a red two-lobed wattle under a small yellow-orange beak. Frog legs and frog
  feet, long toes ending in small yellow claws, and two small useless tufts of
  cream-white feathers at the flanks where wings would be - trim, not wings, and
  they lift nothing. Its eyes are large white spheres set high and wide, each with a
  small black dot pupil and a fine dark rim - no iris colour, no ring.
  Take the build straight off the sheet. It is the spec, not an approximation.
  <Picture 2> THE FROG.  <Picture 3> THE HEN.
  <Picture 5> THE RESEARCHER, seen from behind, which is how he is always seen.

  <Picture 4> is NOT a thing in the scene. It is a colour and construction board:
  the palette, the line weight, and the way the shading grades inside the line. Read the look off it and never draw it.
  <Picture 6> is the pond, <Picture 7> the inside of the hen house. They are
  background paintings that set the colour and the architecture of those places.
  Neither is a character and nothing in them is a subject.

  Take from the character references: the design, the proportions, the colour
  model, the exact shape of comb, wattle, beak, eyes, wings and claws, and the
  palette and light they are painted in.
  Do NOT copy how loosely they are drawn. The references are washier and sketchier
  than the finished film. On screen the outline is a clean bold BLACK line, the
  colour inside it is a flat base softly airbrushed darker toward the rim, and
  every wet rounded surface carries one hard white glint. Tighten them to that.
  What is NOT part of the film is the staging of the sheet: each figure stands on
  a plain pale backdrop with a cast shadow under it. Drop the backdrop and that
  shadow and put the character in the scene. Never reproduce lettering, caption,
  logo, watermark or frame from any reference.

CAST - the reference is the child, and the parents are it taken apart
  EVERY animal in this film has the same eye: a large white sphere with a small
  black dot pupil and a fine dark rim around it. The pupil is the only mark in it.
  That one eye is what makes the frog and the hen look like they belong in the
  same drawing.
  THE FROG - <Picture 2>. The narrator, and the only one who ever speaks. Smooth
  grey-green skin, not warty, broken by soft mottled olive patches, a large pale
  cream belly covering most of his front, the house eyes, long clawed toes, a
  potbellied sitting build, and a wide flat mouth drawn as a single shallow curve. He is what
  <Picture 1> is with every chicken part taken away: NO comb, NO wattle, NO beak,
  NO wings. Permanently wet, with a
  visible sheen. He wears nothing and owns nothing. Earnest, lovesick, entirely
  without shame.
  THE HEN - <Picture 3>. A warm sandy-tan farmyard hen - not dark brown - with a
  cream breast and belly and darker tan on the back and tail, carrying exactly the
  chicken parts that <Picture 1> borrowed: the same red comb, the same dark red
  wattle, the same yellow-orange beak, the same scaly yellow legs and claws. Otherwise completely
  ordinary as a hen, and grand about it: refined, high-nosed, arrogant. She is not
  oblivious, she is above him, which is a different thing and reads differently.
  HER ARC, and it is the spine of the film: up to the moment the grain lands on his
  back she never grants him a deliberate look - she raises her head, judges the
  pond with contempt and walks off, and everything else that reads as a response is
  coincidence, she was pecking anyway. From the grain onward she does look, on
  purpose: she stops dead, she stoops, she pecks him, their eyes meet, she works it
  out, and she offers him her back. That turn happens ONCE and it is earned. Never
  play it early. The house eyes, exactly as the frog has them. No
  eyelashes, no blush, no anthropomorphism of any kind.
  THE FEDERPHIBIEN - the imagined children: <Picture 1> exactly, in numbers.
  Comb, wattle, beak, a squat potbellied frog body, frog feet with small claws,
  small feather tufts at the flanks. The wings
  cannot lift them and they will not swim, and they do not know it yet. They
  appear ONLY inside the chorus fantasies, never in the real pond.
  THE RESEARCHER - <Picture 5>. The only human being in the film, and the film never shows his
  face. Khaki trousers, a plain shirt with the sleeves turned back, a straw hat.
  A green hardbound notebook and a pencil, and an old 1920s camera - a squarish
  black leather-covered folding plate camera - hanging at his front from a broad
  black strap worn across one shoulder. It reads as a dark block and it is easy to
  mistake for a bag; it is not a bag. For almost the whole film only the broad black
  strap across his back and shoulder is really visible, the body and lens only at
  the edge of the close shots of the notebook, and properly just once, at the very
  end, when he finally uses it. Middle aged, unhurried, completely absorbed. He sits on a folding
  chair at the edge of the pond and draws what he sees, in the fine pencil manner
  of a nineteenth century scientific plate, the parts labelled in a small hand.
  Always seen from behind or over his shoulder, at most a rear three-quarter where
  the edge of a nose and a chin show past the hat brim. His face is never turned to
  camera and never readable. Never a reaction shot, never a word. He does not interact with the animals and they never notice
  him. He moves quickly exactly once, when he jumps up and grabs the chair to
  stop it going over.
  He is ALREADY THERE from the very first frame of the film, sitting at the far
  edge of the pond. This is only so that nobody wonders where he came from when the
  song reaches him: he is a plant, not a presence. A wide shot holding the pond bank
  MAY carry him somewhere at the edge of frame, and the least is enough - a khaki
  shape under a straw hat, or just his shadow and the shadow of the chair falling
  across the mud. He is never the subject, never lit for and never looked at, and
  plenty of setups simply do not include him. What is not optional: whenever he is
  in frame at all, he is seen FROM BEHIND. Never his face, at any size, however
  small he is. Indoors and in close-ups he is not in frame.

WORLD
  A murky farm pond at the bottom edge of a chicken yard. High summer, humid,
  overcast bright. Green scum water, cattails, duckweed. A leaning chicken-wire
  fence with a gap worn under it. A coop roofed in corrugated tin, whose INSIDE
  is quite different: tall walls of green-painted vertical timber boarding framed
  by heavy warm-brown posts and beams, an ochre earth floor, a straw nesting shelf
  up under the roof, cables strung across and one bare bulb hanging off a sagging
  one. Never tin on the inside. Dry mud cracked into plates. Flies everywhere.
  The farm plainly works - the coop is repaired, there is feed on the ground -
  and nobody is home. The one person who ever appears is the researcher at the
  pond, and he is never shown from the front. Never explain either of those.

SCALE - these are real animals and they are not the same size
  The references are portraits: every character is drawn alone, filling its own
  frame, on a plain backdrop. Nothing in them says how big anything is. Size
  comes from this list and from nowhere else. Never size a character by how much
  of its reference image it fills.

  THE FROG      a common European pond frog. 8 cm from nose to rump, about
                  10 cm sitting up. The length of a man's thumb. He fits inside
                  a cupped human hand with room to spare.
  THE FEDERPHIBIEN  the same squat frog body with a comb on top, about
                  12 cm to the top of the comb. Still a small animal.
  THE HEN        an ordinary farmyard hen. 45 cm to the top of the comb,
                  50 cm beak to tail. Four to five times the frog's height.
                  Standing beside her he reaches her hock - the backward joint
                  above her foot - and no higher. To bring her beak to him she
                  must fold her neck right down to the ground.
  THE RESEARCHER    an adult man, 180 cm standing, his eye about 120 cm up when
                  he is sitting on the folding chair. Eighteen times the frog,
                  four times the hen. The hen comes to just below his knee. The
                  frog would sit on his palm.
  A FLY           8 mm. A tenth of the frog's body length, a speck beside him.

  PLACES, to the same ruler
    The pond is a farm pond, about 10 m across - the frog needs a couple of
    hundred of his own body lengths to cross it, and the whole of his journey
    from the water to the hen house is roughly 2 m of ground.
    The farmyard is about 40 m across and holds the house, the barn, the coop,
    the fenced run and the pond. Seen from the air the pond is one small dark
    oval in the corner of it and the frog in the water is a single dark speck.
    The hen house is a proper timber barn rather than a low coop - about 3.5 m
    to the ridge, so its doorway stands taller than a man - with a chicken
    pop-hole about 35 cm square and a battened plank ladder up to it. Inside,
    the hen is a small animal against a big wall and that wall runs well above
    her, and the frog is small against the hen.

  HOW THIS SHOWS ON SCREEN
    In any frame holding both the frog and the hen, she is at least four times
    his height, whatever the angle - a low shot may exaggerate her, none may
    shrink her.
    In any frame holding both the frog and the researcher, the frog is at most
    a twentieth of the man's height.
    In any frame holding the whole farm, the frog is a dark mark of a few
    pixels, not a readable animal. He only becomes readable when the framing is
    tight enough to hold the pond alone or less.
    A close-up enlarges a character in the frame. It never enlarges the animal:
    the things around it - grass blades, gravel, feathers, a corn kernel - grow
    with it in the same proportion.

RUNNING GAGS
  The frog is always wet. Wherever he sits a small dark puddle forms, and it is
  still there after he moves away.
  Flies orbit his head in every single beat and he never eats one - he sings that
  he could and is not hungry. He eats exactly one fly in the whole film, offhand,
  riding on her back near the end, and that is the gag paying itself off. Nowhere
  else.
  One single corn kernel keeps coming back - dropped, carried, offered, ignored.
  The hen never blinks at the same time as he does.
  The film never floats a symbol above a character to tell you what it feels - no
  pictograms, no emblems, no comic-strip shorthand of any kind hanging in the air.
  Feeling is carried by the pose, the eyeline and the held beat, nothing else.
  Exactly one shot in the whole film breaks this, and that shot says so itself.

RULES
  Only the frog speaks, and only in the song's own words. Keep his lines in
  German exactly as written.
  The hen makes real chicken sounds and nothing else, and she is never given a
  human reaction or a comedy double-take. She is still allowed to notice, to judge
  and to decide - as a hen would, in the turn of a head and the set of an eye. The
  one moment she is permitted something sharper is the realisation, where her
  pupils contract to pinpricks.
  The frog never looks into the lens until the final beat.
  Nothing here is cute. Everything is sincere. The sincerity is the joke - so
  never play it for laughs, never mug, never wink.
